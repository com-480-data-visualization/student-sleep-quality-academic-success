Milestone 3

How sleep quality
shapes student grades.

We asked 996 students about their sleep, stress, screen time, caffeine, and grades. Nearly all of them sleep seven hours or more — yet almost half rate their nights poor, nine in ten feel chronic stress, and only four percent call their grades good. Scroll to see why hours don't tell the whole story, then explore the data yourself.

The numbers behind the paradox

Each dot is one of the 996 students. As you scroll, watch the same population re-arrange itself by sleep duration, sleep quality, stress, and academic performance — the relationships look nothing like you'd expect.

Step 1 of 6

996 students.

Every dot is one survey response. We start with the whole population — undergraduates and graduate students, of every gender and year of study. Scroll to slice them apart.

Step 2 of 6

They sleep enough.

Grouped by hours per night, 90% land at 7–8 hours or more. Only 38 students fall under six hours. This is not a tired population. Right?

Step 3 of 6

But their sleep is terrible.

Recolour by self-rated quality. 46% rate it "very poor" or "poor"; only 47% rate it "good" or better — and a chunk of the "7–8 hours" cohort sits inside that orange "very poor" band.

Step 4 of 6

And they're stressed.

Swap the colour to stress. The orange overwhelms the green: 93% report "high" or "extremely high" stress. Stress is not the exception in this dataset — it is the rule.

Step 5 of 6

The outcomes are bad.

Recolour by self-rated academic performance. 88% say "poor" or "below average". Only 12% reach "average" — and a vanishing 4% report "good" or "excellent".

Step 6 of 6

So hours don't explain it.

Re-grouped by duration once more, but coloured by performance. Notice the bars look identical: more sleep does not mean better grades. The story isn't hours — it's quality, stress, and lifestyle. Scroll on to explore them.

Takeaways
4

Three things to leave with

01

Hours are not the same as rest.

90% of students hit 7+ hours, yet 46% rate their sleep poor and 88% report concentration problems. Duration is a poor predictor of perceived performance; sleep quality is a much sharper signal.

02

Stress and screens travel together.

Students who rate their sleep "very poor" cluster sharply at the top of the stress and screen-time axes — the lifestyle factors a student can actually change.

03

The good sleepers look different.

"Very good" sleepers exercise more, caffeinate less, and report dramatically lower fatigue and assignment impact — the few who do well across the board.

About this project

Final milestone of EPFL COM-480 — Data Visualization. We rebuilt the Milestone 2 prototype on the real dataset (996 student survey responses) and added a guided martini-glass narrative: a single scrollytelling sequence opens into an unconstrained dashboard.

All charts are written in vanilla D3 v7 modules. No build step, no framework.
